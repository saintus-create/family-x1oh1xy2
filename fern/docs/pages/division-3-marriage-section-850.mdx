presumption arising from the form of title, a common law presumption, is inapplicable when it conflicts with the transmutation statutes.
Justice Chin’s concurrence in Valli further considers the relationship
between the Evidence Code section 622 common law title presumption and California community property law.
In re Marriage of Begian & Sarajian, 31 Cal.App.5th 506, 242
Cal. Rptr.3d 692 (2018), reversed a trial court finding that a transfer
deed validly relinquished a husband’s community property ownership
interest in property when the deed was entitled “Trust Transfer
Deed,” suggesting that the husband may have only intended to grant
his legal, but not his beneficial, interest in the property or that he was
only preparing a document for estate planning purposes.
Invoking Valli, In re Marriage of Lafkas, 237 Cal.App.4th 921, 188
Cal.Rptr.3d 484 (2015), review denied, held that the husband’s
separate property interest in a partnership agreement was not
transmuted to community property despite a modification of the
agreement, signed by the husband, that named husband and wife as
owners of the partnership interest, because the modification did not
contain an express declaration that the character of ownership was
being changed.

Transmutation by agreement or transfer
Subject to Sections 851 to 853, inclusive, married persons
may by agreement or transfer, with or without consideration,
do any of the following:
<span class="subsec">(a)</span>  Transmute community property to separate property
of either spouse.
<span class="subsec">(b)</span>  Transmute separate property of either spouse to
community property.
<span class="subsec">(c)</span>  Transmute separate property of one spouse to separate
property of the other spouse. (Stats.1992, c. 162 (A.B.2650),
§ 10, operative Jan. 1, 1994.)
